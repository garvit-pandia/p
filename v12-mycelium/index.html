01 · the farm

One organism, many fruiting bodies

Dhingri is what an oyster mushroom is called in the hills, and it is where we started: forty bags of straw in a disused apple store in 2018. Solan is cool, damp and a little short of light, which is exactly what a mushroom wants. Today three growing rooms and a small lab turn out about 900 kg a week. Nothing is sprayed, nothing is flown, and nothing waits in a cold store. A mushroom is the fruit of something much larger that lives in the block and never leaves the farm. We look after that part, and it does the rest.

    03 · kitchen notes

    Spores of advice, free with every box

    01

    Do not wash themA dry brush or a damp cloth. Water is what makes mushrooms slimy.

    02

    Hot pan, then fatDry-fry for two minutes first so they brown before they stew.

    03

    Do not crowd the panOne layer, with gaps. Cook in two batches if you have to.

    04

    Salt at the endSalt early and they give up their water and boil in it.

    05

    Paper bag, not plasticIn the fridge door, not the drawer. They need to breathe.

    06

    Tear oysters by handAlong the gills. Ragged edges catch more of the pan.

    07

    Keep the stemsShiitake and king trumpet stems make a stock in twenty minutes.

    08

    Lion’s mane likes a weightPress it under a second pan for the first three minutes.

    09

    Past their best?Slice thin, dry in a low oven, and grind into a seasoning.

    10

    They freeze, cookedFry first, cool, then freeze flat. Never freeze them raw.

    11

    Sunlight helpsTwenty minutes gills-up on a windowsill raises their vitamin D.

    04 · how it grows

    Growth rings

    Day 0 — The substrate (wheat straw and oak sawdust)

    Straw from farms in the valley is chopped, soaked and steamed for six hours to clear out everything that is not a mushroom.

    Day 1 — Inoculation (grain spawn, mixed in by hand)

    We fold in spawn raised in our own lab, bag it, and move the blocks to a dark room held at twenty-four degrees.

    Days 2 to 21 — The run (nothing to see, everything happening)

    The mycelium threads through the block until the whole thing turns white. We check it and otherwise leave it alone.

    Days 22 to 26 — Pinning (cold air, light and mist)

    A drop to sixteen degrees and a little daylight tells the block it is autumn. Pins the size of match heads appear overnight.

    Days 27 to 30 — Harvest (picked at five in the morning)

    They double in size every day. We pick by hand at dawn, pack in paper, and the van leaves for the plains at eight.
